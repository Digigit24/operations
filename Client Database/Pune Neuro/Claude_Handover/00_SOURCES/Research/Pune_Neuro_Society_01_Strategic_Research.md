## SECTION 2 — MARKET & CATEGORY ANALYSIS

### The category: Regional / city-anchored medical societies in India

Medical societies in India operate across three tiers:

1. **National apex bodies** (NSI, IAN) — set agenda, run flagship national conferences, publish journals.
2. **Specialty sub-societies** (Movement Disorders Society of India, Indian Stroke Association, etc.) — deep vertical focus.
3. **Regional / city societies** (Bombay Neuro, NSP, Delhi Neurological Association) — local academic community + conference hosting on the national circuit.

NSP sits firmly in tier 3, with rising ambition to host national-scale meetings (IANCON 2011, ICTRIMS 2026).

### Audience & demand patterns

- **Primary audience:** practising neurologists and neurosurgeons in Pune (members) + neurology PGs in Pune institutions.
- **Secondary audience:** Indian neurology community at large (delegates to NSP-hosted national meetings).
- **Tertiary audience:** allied specialists (radiologists, intensivists, psychiatrists, neuroscientists) and, increasingly, the public via awareness programming.

### Demand drivers for society membership / engagement

- CME credit / academic continuing education.
- Peer presence and case-discussion habit.
- Access to global thought leadership at affordable proximity.
- Networking and clinical referral webs.
- Prestige association — being seen as part of a serious academic community.

### What's NOT a demand driver (and shouldn't be marketed as one)

- ❌ Patient acquisition for members. NSP is not a marketing vehicle for member clinics. This must be a non-negotiable.
- ❌ Pharma or device commercial promotion. Sponsorship may exist but academic independence must be preserved in messaging.
- ❌ Local consumer health information. Public awareness is one workstream, but it's not the society's core identity.

### Buying objections (member / delegate side)

- "Another meeting? I already have NSI, IAN, my hospital CME, and online courses."
- "I'm too busy clinically to attend monthly meets."
- "What do I actually get out of membership beyond a certificate?"
- "Is the academic quality worth the registration fee?" (for conferences)

### Decision triggers

- A specific faculty member they want to learn from (e.g., "Montalban is speaking, I'm going").
- A case-discussion topic relevant to their current clinical work.
- Peer pressure (their hospital lead is going, the PG batch is going).
- PG examination relevance.
- Prestige of association (being part of a Pune neurology community helps career identity).

### Market saturation

National neurology conference circuit is full (IANCON, MDSI annual, ISA, regional meets, hospital-led CMEs). The differentiator is **format quality** and **convening power**, not "another conference." NSP's Approach Meet format (case-led, interactive, PG-friendly) is its distinctive product.

### Trust factors specific to medical societies

- Heritage of office bearers and patrons.
- Quality of faculty (international + national).
- Consistency of programming (15-year track record matters).
- Independence from commercial influence.
- Endorsement by apex bodies (IAN sub-section partnership for ICTRIMS = strong signal).

### Positioning gap visible

Most Indian regional neurology societies look and feel identical — generic medical-association websites, standard navigation, faculty grids, no digital knowledge layer. The gap is **"a city society that behaves like an institution"**: meaningful permanent assets (lecture archive, case library, PG curriculum, public health explainers), distinctive visual identity, modern digital presence.

That's where NSP can win.

## SECTION 7 — AUDIENCE PROFILES (peer-focused, not patient-focused)

Five core profiles. Each is a member / engagement target, not a patient.

### Profile 1 — The Senior Consultant (member, anchor)

- Mid-to-late career neurologist or neurosurgeon practising in Pune.
- 15+ years of clinical experience, established practice or hospital appointment.
- Identifies strongly with the Pune medical community.
- Engages with NSP for: peer presence, hosting / chairing case discussions, prestige association, mentoring PGs.
- Pain: time-pressured; needs efficient academic value per hour spent.
- What works: high-signal programmes, named oration invitations, leadership roles.
- What fails: low-quality content, transactional outreach, anything that feels like a hospital advertisement.
- Best content: discussion panels, named orations, expert-to-expert debates.

### Profile 2 — The Mid-Career Neurologist (member, engaged)

- 5–15 years post-DM/MCh.
- Building reputation; presenting at conferences; deepening sub-specialty interest.
- Engages with NSP for: case-presentation opportunities, CME credit, exposure to international faculty, sub-specialty programming.
- Pain: balancing private practice + academic ambition.
- What works: structured case-discussion formats, sub-specialty deep dives, named-presenter slots.
- What fails: undifferentiated programming, ego-bruising panel formats, weak moderation.

### Profile 3 — The Neurology PG (audience, future member)

- DM Neurology or MCh Neurosurgery resident, typically at Pune institutions (BJ, AFMC, Sancheti, Ruby Hall, etc.).
- High learning hunger, exam-driven, future-society-member.
- Engages with NSP for: PG Approach Meeting, lecture archive, exam-relevant content, mentorship visibility.
- Pain: information overload; needs curated, exam-aligned material.
- What works: PG-aimed explainers, recorded lectures, "approach to X" content, case-based learning.
- What fails: content pitched only at consultants, paywalls on educational content, slow page load.

### Profile 4 — The National / International Faculty Member

- Senior neurologist invited as a speaker.
- Engages with NSP for: chance to address a serious Indian audience, network with Indian KOLs, contribute to a respected programme.
- Pain: time, travel, demands on schedule.
- What works: well-run programmes, strong moderation, high-quality interaction, recorded outputs they can share.
- What fails: disorganised logistics, weak audience interaction, no follow-through after the event.

### Profile 5 — The Concerned Public Member (occasional audience)

- Family member of a patient with a neurological condition, seeking authoritative information.
- Engages with NSP via: public awareness content, social media, occasional event coverage.
- Pain: medical information is overwhelming and untrustworthy online.
- What works: clear, society-vetted explainers; framing as public-good content, not personal advice.
- What fails: anything that looks like a referral to a specific member's practice; anything that crosses into individualised medical guidance.

**Crucial:** Profile 5 is small, careful, and intentionally limited. Do not over-index on this audience — it risks diluting NSP's academic identity.

## SECTION 13 — COMPLIANCE & POSITIONING GUARDRAILS

NSP is a society of medical professionals. The compliance posture differs from a clinic.

### What applies

- **NMC Code of Medical Ethics** — applies to all member doctors and to society communications that involve member doctors. Society messaging must not solicit patients for member practices.
- **Society Act compliance** — governance, election, and reporting norms under the Societies Registration Act apply.
- **Pharma / industry sponsorship transparency** — when sponsors appear at NSP events (e.g., Sponsored Symposium at ICTRIMS), disclosure is good practice in academic content.

### What is less directly applicable (vs. a clinic)

- **ASCI advertising code** — applies only if NSP runs ad campaigns; minimal day-to-day relevance.
- **Drugs & Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act** — applies only if any content promises specific cures or treatments for listed conditions. Easy to avoid by maintaining academic framing.

### Hard guardrails for all NSP content

1. No content shall function as a referral to any specific member doctor's clinic or practice.
2. No content shall claim specific treatment outcomes for individual patients.
3. No content shall make commercial promises (cure, guarantee, "best in Pune," etc.).
4. Public-awareness content shall always include a clear "consult your doctor" framing.
5. Sponsored content must be transparently labelled.
6. All clinical / public-health content must be reviewed by qualified society members before publication.
